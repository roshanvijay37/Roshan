Thirty directions.
Tell me which one.

Each is your real content — the same five projects, three services and fifteen tools — in a different design language taken from what is currently winning on Awwwards. Open them on your phone and scroll properly. Judge the feel; every one of them is rough and none is finished.

D1

Editorial Brutalist

A newspaper plate. Enormous uppercase type, hard rules, a numbered index instead of picture cards. Serif italic accents in gold. Hovering a project turns its name italic and slides a colour swatch in.

Feels: confident, expensive, design-literate. Risk: austere — no images at all.
D2

Dark Cinematic

Near-black with blurred gold gradient blobs drifting on scroll, film grain over everything, gradient text, lines that rise into place. Project cards glow where your cursor is.

Feels: premium, moody, agency. Risk: the most common look on Awwwards.
D3

Horizontal Scroll

Work pins to the screen and travels sideways as you scroll down, with a progress bar and a live counter. Light and airy everywhere else.

Feels: playful, technical, memorable. Risk: some people fight the scroll.
D4

Split Sticky

Your name, pitch and WhatsApp button pinned on the left forever; work scrolls past on the right. The contact button never leaves the screen.

Feels: editorial, calm, businesslike. Best for: actually getting hired.
D5

Full-screen Deck

Every project owns an entire screen in its own colour, with snap scrolling that clicks from one to the next. Condensed poster type, soft blurred shapes, and the browser chrome tints to match the panel you are on.

Feels: bold, poster-like, impossible to skim past. Risk: five screens of scrolling before contact.
D6

Spotlight Reveal

A torch follows your finger and lights a hidden gold layer underneath — one message on top, a different one beneath. On phone it orbits by itself and follows your drag. Project rows wash with their colour.

Feels: experimental, memorable, a bit of theatre. Risk: gimmick if overused.
D7

Bento Grid

Everything is a tile — hero, live-project count, each project, each service, the WhatsApp card, a ticker of the stack. Two columns on phone, six on desktop. The most current layout on this list.

Feels: modern, product-like, scannable in seconds. Best for: people who skim.
D8

Kinetic Type

No sections — one continuous scroll where type is the animation. The hero splits apart as you leave it, then each project title tracks across the screen while its block is pinned, with a colour rule filling underneath.

Feels: "more animation than website" — which is what you asked for. Risk: the least conventional.
D9

Spotlight + Deck

D5 and D6 combined, and the one you have been looking at. The pinned headline splits apart on scroll, the spotlight panel makes its claim, then every project takes a full screen in its own colour. No counts anywhere.

Feels: like a statement followed by proof. Note: the headline is still generic.
D10

Preloader + Overlay Menu

Opens on a counter that fills to 100 and then wipes away to reveal the site. The menu is a full-screen overlay that irises open from the button, with links at headline size. Project rows wash with their colour.

Feels: like a studio site, staged and deliberate. Risk: a preloader delays real content.
D11

Live Screens

Every project sits inside a browser window with its real URL in the address bar, tilting upright as it enters. The headline is "Don't read about it. Go and click it."

Feels: like proof rather than a pitch. Best for: a client asking "what have you built?"
D12

Archive Index

Your work as a sortable table — project, kind, built with, link. Tap a column heading to reorder it. Dense, factual, no decoration.

Feels: like someone with a body of work, not a vibe. Risk: plain, if you want to look designed.
D13

Terminal

The site is a shell. It types whoami and work on load, and the commands along the bottom are the navigation — services, stack, contact, clear.

Feels: unmistakably a developer's site. Risk: non-technical clients may find it cold.
D14

Cursor Gallery

Enormous project names; the rest dim while the one under your cursor stays lit, and a preview panel chases the pointer. On a phone there is no hover, so the first tap opens the detail in place and the second follows the link.

Feels: gallery, curated, confident. Risk: the best of it only exists on desktop.
D15

Draggable Board

Nothing scrolls. Your work is pinned to an infinite surface and you drag it around — projects, services, stack and contact scattered across it. Different verb from every other direction here.

Feels: playful, tactile, genuinely memorable. Risk: people expect to scroll.
D16

Stacking Cards

Each project sticks to the screen and the next slides over it, shrinking the one beneath. The deck literally stacks as you read, so what you have already seen stays visible underneath.

Feels: current, smooth, product-like. Risk: the most fashionable, so the fastest to date.
D17

Swiss Poster

Print, not screen. Flat colour fields, 2px rules, hard corners, no shadows or gradients anywhere — each project is a full-bleed colour block with its name set enormous.

Feels: bold, graphic, art-directed. Risk: loud; the colour does the talking.
D18

Dither

A 1-bit world — one ink on one paper, a halftone dot screen over everything, pixel type, halftone swatches instead of screenshots. Nothing else in this set looks remotely like it.

Feels: distinctive, technical, has a point of view. Risk: a strong taste to commit to.
D19

Marquee Wall

Every row is a ticker running at its own speed and direction, and they all speed up when you scroll fast. Hollow outline type against solid, nothing ever quite still.

Feels: loud, energetic, restless. Risk: hard to read anything calmly.
D20

Split-flap

An airport departures board. Every heading shuffles through the alphabet before landing on its letters, one board at a time as you scroll to it.

Feels: mechanical, charming, precise. Risk: the trick is spent after one visit.
D21

Conversation

The whole site is a chat thread — you ask, I answer, projects arrive as link cards. Your contact button is already WhatsApp; this puts the entire page in that register.

Feels: personal, disarming, easy to read. Best for: clients who message rather than browse.
D22

Neubrutalist

3px black borders and hard offset shadows, no blur anywhere. Bright flats — amber, coral, blue. Cards you could pick up off the page.

Feels: confident, current, fun. Risk: very of-the-moment.
D23

Aurora

Painted colour fields drifting on canvas behind frosted glass panels. Gradient text, film grain, everything soft.

Feels: premium, calm, expensive. Risk: the closest to a generic AI-startup look.
D24

Broadsheet

A newspaper. Serif throughout, a drop cap, three columns of work, rules between sections. No motion at all.

Feels: literate, considered, grown-up. Risk: quiet; nothing moves.
D25

Desktop

Not a page — a room. Windows you drag by their title bars, a dock along the bottom that brings each to the front.

Feels: inventive, technical, memorable. Risk: people have to work out how to use it.
D26

Minimal

One column, one serif, no cards, no motion, nothing to hover. The argument is that the work does not need help.

Feels: senior, quietly certain. Risk: reads as unfinished if the work is not strong.
D27

Coverflow

The projects are a stack seen at an angle. Scroll and they rotate through, the front one upright and readable, the rest receding.

Feels: tactile, dimensional, playful. Risk: only one project readable at a time.
D28

Blueprint

Drafting paper — grid, dimension lines, a spec column beside each project listing URL, stack and status.

Feels: engineered, precise, credible. Best for: convincing someone technical.
D29

Ledger

A bound book with ruled lines and a red margin, each project written in as an entry. Warm and analogue.

Feels: crafted, unhurried, human. Risk: nostalgic, which may not suit software.
D30

Skew Scroll

The page reacts to how fast you scroll — content skews and lags with your velocity, then springs back when you stop. There is a live readout in the corner.

Feels: alive, physical, responsive. Risk: motion-heavy; some find it queasy.
